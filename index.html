Andrew Wingate  ·  Evezor Inc.  ·  wafer.space

Hi, I'm Andrew Wingate with Evezor. This is a proposal for the back end of open silicon: everything that happens to a chip after the wafer comes out of the fab. I call it the last mile.

It's also about trust: knowing who has held a chip, being able to prove where it came from, and being able to tell it from a copy.

Open silicon has solved the front end, design and fab. Getting chips off the wafer and onto a board is still the hard part, and that's what this is about.

MEME Chip

This is MEME. It's on wafer.space's GF180 run, about 3.9 by 2.5 millimeters, with doge art on every metal layer. I have 2,000 of them.

The interesting part is hidden in the art. Look at the six dogecoin medallions I've circled: under each one is a cluster of eight resistor dividers. That's 48 PUF taps per die, and each one is routed out to its own pad so I can probe it directly.

I was telling bunnie about this chip: how stupid it is, and what some of the pieces were actually built for. He just laughed and said, "This is usually how these things start."

Let's zoom in on the lower one on the left.

Real die, under the microscope · PUF cluster is the lavender block under the coin

Eight dividers under every coin.

  • The whole cluster is 80 × 45 µm, about the width of a human hair.
  • The art is carved away around it, so it hides in plain sight.

This is one medallion on a real die, photographed under my microscope. The yellow ring and the coin in the middle are the doge art in metal.

The lavender block under the coin is the PUF cluster: eight dividers, each a pair of poly resistors, sitting on a shared high and low rail. On the right is the same cluster drawn from the layout. The whole thing is 80 by 45 microns, about the width of a human hair. The eight tap lines and the two rails run off to the side, out to the pads.

The art is carved away around it, so on the finished die it just looks like part of the coin.

PUF48 passive dividers · read unpowered

A fingerprint made of resistors.

  • Ratio rk cancels process spread and temperature.
  • What's left: tiny per-die random mismatch.
~240 bits / die simulated: design estimate, no silicon read yet

Each tap is the middle of a two-resistor divider. The chip is completely passive for this: no power, you just force one volt across the rails and read 48 voltages.

Taking the ratio cancels process spread and temperature drift. What's left is the random mismatch between resistors, and that's the fingerprint.

The bars on the right are simulated. Each "read" is a different die. The design estimate is about 4 to 5 bits per tap, around 240 bits per die. That's an estimate: no silicon has been measured yet.

ViabilityThe uncertainty

Are these PUFs truly viable?

This kind of PUF could get less reliable as processes improve.Tim Ansell, wafer.space (paraphrased)

Better processes mean tighter matching, and tighter matching means less mismatch to fingerprint. Nobody has the data to say how much is left.

Less spread means fewer bits per tap. How much spread is there today? Unknown.

Here's the uncertainty. In an earlier conversation with you and Tim Ansell, Tim pointed out that this type of PUF might become unreliable as manufacturing improves. Tighter processes mean better-matched resistors, so less randomness to work with.

He may be right. I don't know, and neither does anyone else, because the data doesn't exist yet.

Here's the simplest way to say what MEME is: every chip is an NFT you can hold. A non-fungible token, but the token is the physical die.

To be clear about scope: I'm not trying to store keys with this. That's a much harder problem, and it's what Cloneless works on. The goals are uniqueness, meaning no two dies alike, and anti-forgery, meaning you can't fake one.

Why is this form valuable? A normal NFT is a record that points at a file, and anyone can copy the file. Here the uniqueness is the object itself, set by random variation when the wafer was made. There's no secret inside to steal: the fingerprint can be published, because what makes it valuable is that nobody can build a die that matches it. Checking it takes a meter, with the chip unpowered, so you don't have to trust any firmware. And it's nearly free: standard resistors hidden under the art, about two tenths of a percent of the die.

That identity follows the chip through every step of the supply chain, which comes back later in the talk.

So the answer is data. I have 2,000 chips with 48 taps each: 96,000 measurements.

We read every one and publish the whole dataset openly, so anyone can run their own analysis: Hamming distance, bias, spatial correlation, randomness suites.

Characterizing MEME at scale also produces the noise and entropy data a fuzzy extractor needs. Key protection isn't my goal, but this dataset gives the people working on it, like Cloneless, real numbers to design against.

GF180 is a mature node, so this becomes the baseline for tracking whether these PUFs hold up. That's tier A on the ladder.

But nobody reads 2,000 chips by hand. The moment we build a rig to do it, we've crossed into B: that same rig becomes the standard tester for every wafer.space chip, with the PUF read as an add-on.

And measuring these isn't only about the PUF. There are generic testing procedures every chip needs, whatever it is. I'll get to those in the coming slides.

What the Baochip needs won't come from a spec sheet. It shows up as we build.

The goal: a low-cost way to test many designs.
Such any designer can write or make their own tests.

Here's what testing looks like when it's built for open silicon. One tester for MEME and for every wafer.space chip: a socket with swappable inserts. A chip drops in, no soldering, spring probes touch every contact, and you find out.

The baseline test is the same for everyone. Every pad has an ESD diode, so we push a little current through each contact: about 0.6 volts means it's made and correct. No current means open; the wrong voltage means a misbond or a short. It doesn't need to know anything about the design.

On top of that, each chip runs what it needs. For MEME, the PUF read, and that's where the 96,000 measurements come from. For a wafer.space design, its own functional tests. And it isn't only wafer.space: chips from other shuttles, in other packages, take the same rig.

bunnie's Baochip is a different job. Solder bumps instead of bond pads, his firmware, his customers' keys. I won't claim this exact rig is his tester. But building it is the path to his. The contact check, the probe inserts, the test software and the logging all carry over. And what the Baochip really needs beyond that, I expect to find out by building, not by guessing from a spec today.

Every shuttle carries dozens of designs, and behind every one of them is someone waiting to find out if their chip works. This is how they find out, before anything is soldered down.

Custody & traceability

Every handoff is a trust boundary.

die swapcounterfeit / re-markgrey-market overruns tamperingfaked testskey leakslost identity

Bunnie built IRIS to check chips after packaging. Vertical integration means you don't have to defend against your own supply chain.

Every time a product passes to another vendor, you add a trust boundary, and each one is an attack surface: die swaps, counterfeits, grey-market overruns, tampering, faked test results, leaked keys.

You all know bunnie built IRIS to verify silicon after the fact. That's how little the chain can be trusted. Owning the steps removes most of those handoffs. On MEME, the PUF is the identity, so the die can vouch for itself.

Where the savings come from

Keeping costs low.

336 handoffs6N · N = 56 designs
60N+4

We don't cut prices. We cut steps, and that only works when one operator owns all of them.

This is the life of a die after the wafer, and the two ways to run it. Sort splits the wafer into N bins, one per design.

On top is the partitioned route, the way it's done today. Every design travels alone: N reels, N mounting jobs, a bonder that has to keep N bins apart, N package lots, N test lots, N shipments. Each arrow bundle is N separate moves. That's 6N handoffs, 336 for our 56 designs.

On the bottom is the mixed route. Dies go straight from sort onto boards and get marked there, so the tape step disappears. After that everything moves as one stream: one bonding job, one packaging job, one test run for every part. Only when we pack for shipping do we read the marks and split them back out by design. That's N plus 4 moves: 60.

The white dots are where a customer can take delivery. For Tim and wafer.space, two of them matter. Bare die off the tape works, but the pads are 75 microns, so customers need precision equipment. A tested package can go on any standard SMT line, and that's what makes open silicon usable for an ordinary hardware designer.

This is where our cost advantage comes from. We're not planning to win by simply charging less than everyone else; that isn't a way to run a business. The savings come from the way the operation runs: fewer handoffs, fewer jobs, fewer chances for something to go wrong. And you can only run it this way when one operator owns every step.
